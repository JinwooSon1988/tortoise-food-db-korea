Bur clover

Medicago polymorpha L.
Also known as · Medicago hispida, burr medic, toothed medick
BFeed with conditions

Can be part of a mixed diet within the stated limits. Do not let one plant dominate the diet.

Why this assessmentWhen correctly identified as Medicago polymorpha, it can be used in limited amounts in a varied mixed diet of wild plants. The plant currently recorded as Medicago hispida is treated as Medicago polymorpha, and there is a record of wild Testudo graeca graeca actually eating this taxon. That wild study does not, however, establish the exact part eaten or a captive feeding proportion. A separate plant-chemistry study found saponin-type compounds in the aerial parts of this species, but it does not show from what amount they become harmful to tortoises. Without overstating “saponins = toxic”, feeding it alone or in a high proportion is avoided and it is limited to being part of a variety of wild plants.

⚠ Read this alongside the safety assessment

Even when a plant has been eaten in the wild, toxicity, antinutrient or veterinary risk information from other animals may also exist. Being eaten in the wild does not by itself make a plant safe in captivity.

  • Saponins — Saponin composition and content have been studied in the aerial parts of annual Medicago, including M. polymorpha.
    The presence of saponins alone does not establish toxicity in tortoises, and the fact that it is eaten in the wild does not mean the risk can be ignored either.
  • Risk factors in forage use — Medicago polymorpha is used as a pasture and forage plant, but in Medicago feed science, risk factors such as saponins, bloat (gas building up in the stomach) and oestrogen-like effects in some lines are also considered.
    It does not directly prove toxicity in tortoises. It is, however, supporting risk evidence that wild feeding records alone cannot justify recommending unlimited feeding.

What would make the conclusion more certain: There is direct wild-feeding evidence in Testudo, but how far the saponin and forage-science risks apply to reptiles is unclear. Publication needs conservative wording no stronger than limited mixed feeding, with the directness of the evidence shown.

What the source says

A field study of the feeding ecology of wild Greek tortoises (T. graeca graeca) living in a dry, overgrazed area of Morocco. The study confirmed a plant then recorded as Medicago hispida (now listed in this database as Medicago polymorpha) in the list of plants actually eaten. It is therefore used as direct field evidence that this plant species was actually part of the natural diet of a particular Greek tortoise subspecies.

Role in this assessment
Directly supports the current feeding conclusion.

What this source cannot show
A wild feeding record from a particular region and subspecies, so it does not mean the same selectivity has been confirmed in other Greek tortoise subspecies or other Testudo species. It is also not a trial that sets the exact part eaten, captive feeding proportion or frequency, a long-term safe amount or suitability as a sole staple.
