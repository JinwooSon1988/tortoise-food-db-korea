DDo not feed

Do not include it in the planned diet. This is a separate question from whether an accidental small bite is toxic.

Why this assessmentA specialist tortoise database classifies perilla as do not feed, and independent research has linked perilla ketone in Perilla frutescens to lung toxicity in several animals. Because chemotypes differ between cultivars, perilla is excluded from planned feeding, but this is not extended to a toxic dose for any particular species or to the same level of risk for every edible perilla-leaf cultivar.

Peer-reviewed paperComposition evidence
What the source says

A toxicology study reporting that perilla ketone, isolated from perilla essential oil, is a potent cause of pulmonary oedema in laboratory animals and livestock. It isolated and confirmed in the plant a causative compound that can explain the lung damage reported in grazing cattle that ate perilla. In other words, the warning signs for perilla are supported not just by husbandry anecdotes but by a specific chemical compound and animal toxicity data.

Role in this assessment
Indirect evidence that supplements the current conclusion. It does not decide safety on its own.

What this source cannot show
Confirming the toxicity of perilla ketone itself is not a measurement of absorption, metabolism or susceptibility when tortoises are fed perilla leaves. A toxic dose, a safe small amount, a feeding frequency for tortoises, or the actual exposure from edible Korean perilla therefore cannot be calculated from these data alone.

Wilson BJ, Garst JE, Linnabary RD, Channell RB. Perilla ketone: a potent lung toxin from the mint plant, Perilla frutescens Britton. Science. 1977;197(4303):573-574.

Peer-reviewed paperRelated-taxon evidence
What the source says

A veterinary toxicology study that fed actual perilla (Perilla frutescens) plants to cattle. Atypical interstitial pneumonia developed in 3 of 5 animals, and post-mortem examination found pulmonary emphysema and oedema. The amounts that caused signs were 2.3–15.5 kg of green plants in the seed stage or 11.8 kg of hay. Precise instrumental analysis (HPLC) found the highest perilla ketone concentrations in parts at the flowering and seed stages, and in the actual feeding trial this growth stage was also the most toxic. It is therefore used as strong veterinary warning evidence that perilla carries a growth-stage-dependent toxicity risk confirmed in real animals.

Role in this assessment
Indirect evidence that supplements the current conclusion. It does not decide safety on its own.

What this source cannot show
The subjects of this study were cattle, not tortoises. The kilogram-scale doses that caused signs in cattle cannot be converted into a toxic or safe amount for tortoises, and this does not mean that every perilla cultivar grown for food in Korea, and every leaf, has the same perilla ketone concentration. Neither the conclusion that “one bite causes poisoning” nor an exact risk dose for tortoises can be drawn from this study alone.

Kerr LA, Johnson BJ, Burrows GE. Intoxication of cattle by Perilla frutescens (purple mint). Veterinary and Human Toxicology. 1986;28(5):412-416.

Peer-reviewed paperComposition evidence
What the source says

A study comparing 5 genotypes of P. frutescens and analysing perilla ketone (PK), phenolics, carotenoids and other compounds. Precise instrumental analysis detected the toxic compound PK in 2 of the 5 genotypes, and the researchers rated those two genotypes unsuitable for eating. Other genotypes had no detectable PK, showing that the level of the hazardous compound can vary greatly with the chemotype and genotype of perilla.

Role in this assessment
Indirect evidence that supplements the current conclusion. It does not decide safety on its own.

What this source cannot show
A composition comparison of 5 genotypes, not a tortoise feeding trial. A particular Korean edible cultivar therefore cannot be called safe without confirming that it lacks PK, nor can all perilla be generalised as having the same PK concentration. It gives no toxicity threshold, safe feeding amount or frequency for tortoises.

Determination of toxic perilla ketone, secondary plant metabolites and antioxidative capacity in five Perilla frutescens L. varieties. Food and Chemical Toxicology. 2010;48(1):264-270.

The specialist database categorizes Perilla as Do not Feed. The assessment combines that husbandry caution with separately stored mammalian toxicology and chemotype evidence while excluding planned feeding.

Role in this assessment
Indirect evidence that supplements the current conclusion. It does not decide safety on its own.
