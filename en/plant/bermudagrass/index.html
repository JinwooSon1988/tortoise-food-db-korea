Bermuda grass

Cynodon dactylon
ARecommended

A good core component of a varied mixed diet of suitable plants.

Why this assessmentA faecal diet analysis of wild Testudo graeca confirmed the leaves and stems of Bermuda grass (Cynodon dactylon) as a major food item actually eaten. This is more direct evidence than general herbivore forage data. Being eaten in the wild is not, however, a trial result showing that it can be fed without limit as a sole staple in captivity, and it does not set captive proportions for any species or subspecies. Leaves and stems not exposed to pesticides or herbicides are therefore used as one ingredient of a mixed diet with several suitable wild plants.

How to feed

Use it as a main part of an everyday mixed diet together with other suitable plants. Avoid feeding only this plant or unlimited amounts.

What the source says

A study that classified a total of 4,422 food fragments in the faeces of 44 wild Greek tortoises (20 adult males, 16 adult females and 8 juveniles) in Algeria. Leaves of Cynodon dactylon made up about 11.9%, 9.0% and 10.0% of faecal fragments in males, females and juveniles respectively, and stems about 2.5%, 2.1% and 2.7%. Across the study, vegetative plant tissue was detected more than reproductive tissue, and C. dactylon was both abundant at the site and one of the main grass foods in the actual diet.

Role in this assessment
Directly supports the current feeding conclusion.

What this source cannot show
The relative frequency of faecal fragments is not the same as a captive feeding proportion or nutritional contribution, and soft tissues may be digested and under-detected. It is a study of one wild population in Algeria, so it does not mean all Greek tortoise populations eat it in the same proportion, and it is not a safety trial showing Bermuda grass can be fed as a sole staple or without limit.
